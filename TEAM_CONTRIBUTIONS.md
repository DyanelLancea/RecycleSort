## Individual reflections
 
**Happy Calista (Authentication & User Management).** Building login showed me that a security control has to be judged where it actually runs. The rate limit blocked at the 6th attempt in our automated test, but on AWS with two servers it only blocked at the 10th, because each server keeps its own in-memory count. If I did it again I would keep the counter in a shared store such as the database, so the limit holds however many servers are running.
 
**Balandra Dyanel Lancea Estillore (Image Upload & Input Validation).** Starting from an AI-generated skeleton saved time, but much of it had to be checked and replaced once the real model and AWS design were chosen. Validating uploads before inference (allow-listed types, a 5 MB limit and a Pillow check) meant a text file renamed to .jpg was rejected with a 400 instead of reaching the model. Keeping the report, manifest and evidence consistent with what was really deployed took more care than I expected.
 
**Chia Ting Hui Wileen (ML Classification & Recyclable Logic).** Comparing ResNet50 with EfficientNetB0 taught me to look at macro F1 and the confusion matrix, not just accuracy: trash, the smallest class, was the weakest (F1 0.87), and most errors were between look-alike materials such as cardboard and paper. I also learned that the more accurate model has a cost in the cloud, because the 225 MB file makes each new server about 10 seconds slower on its first scan.
 
**Felicia Seah Yun Xin (Gamification, Logging & Database).** Moving from SQLite to RDS PostgreSQL showed me why migrations matter: Alembic created the same tables on the cloud database without hand-written SQL. Making /health check the database means a server that cannot reach RDS is taken out of the load balancer instead of returning errors, and the redeploy on 11 Oct showed the scan history survived because the data lives in RDS, not on the servers.
 
**Leong Jia En Melissa (Frontend and AWS deployment).** Deploying on AWS taught me that small configuration details decide whether a design works, such as a placeholder saved in Parameter Store or nginx's default server answering on IPv6. The failure and load tests were the most valuable part, because they turned claims about recovery and scaling into measured results. Redeploying the redesigned frontend also showed a Learner Lab limit: instance refresh was blocked, so I rolled out the new AMI by scaling to four servers and back to two.

## Team Reflection
Looking back, the hardest part was making the report match the app we actually deployed. The repository started as a skeleton, and a lot of the early plan changed once the model and the AWS setup were in place. Before writing this, we went back to the saved tests. A normal upload worked from login through to points, and a file that only pretended to be a photo was rejected. We were pleased with 94.86% on the 1,848 test images, though the mistakes were mostly between similar materials, such as cardboard and paper. The recovery test felt the most real: after one server was stopped, some requests failed for a short time before a replacement became healthy.  The Learner Lab budget panel updated late, but it eventually showed about USD 1.30 spent, which matched our estimate of USD 1.25–1.85. The lab has no certificate, so the site stays on HTTP. The screenshots and exported settings in the repository are what we are leaving as the record of the deployment.
